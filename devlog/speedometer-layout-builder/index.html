Build your own dashboard: the Speedometer layout builder

3 November 2026

Until late October, every layout in Speedometer Live Wallpaper was one we had drawn. Version 2.0.7, out on Google Play on 29 October, hands that job to you: a layout builder where you place the gauges yourself, and a market where you can publish what you make. The next two days went into fixing our own layouts, because the builder made their flaws easy to see. This post covers both.

One frame for every style

The app has 19 styles, from a walnut dashboard to a jet's head-up display, and a layout has to work in all of them. So every style shares one frame: the screen, minus a thin margin at the sides and top and a deeper one at the bottom, so your row of app icons never sits on top of a gauge. A layout is the same size in every style. You can build it in Vintage, switch to Prism and find every widget where you left it.

A layout holds up to 16 widgets, each kind once. The builder is part of the Android app; on the web you can open any published layout on the try page and see it drawn in any style.

Snapping instead of a grid

We tried to avoid a visible grid, because a dashboard isn't a spreadsheet. While you drag a widget, its centre snaps to the middle of the frame or lines up with the centre of another widget, and a thin amber line shows what it caught on. A widget can't be dragged out of the frame. You resize it with a handle, zoom the stage up to four times to place small pieces precisely, and undo or redo any step. With TalkBack on, every widget has move and size actions, so the builder works without dragging at all.

Letting widgets overlap

The first version of the builder refused overlaps. A widget dropped onto another turned red and slid to the nearest free space. It looked tidy, and in practice it fought you: real dashboards tuck a small dial into the edge of a big one. So overlaps came back, with Bring to front and Send to back, and a minimum size for each widget so nothing shrinks into an unreadable dot.

Overlaps brought a new problem: grabbing the widget underneath. That is what Lock is for. A locked widget ignores drags and resizes, wears a small padlock, and lets your finger pass through to whatever lies behind it.

One widget, nineteen looks

There are 42 kinds of widget: the original 17 and 25 new ones, among them a trip computer, an odometer, a 0–100 widget, a G-force meter, the weather, the moon, your steps, music controls and a second clock. Drawing 42 widgets 19 times by hand wasn't realistic, so each style has a kit. It offers the style's own forms first, cut from its real artwork and never stretched, and then a set of plain forms painted in the style's colours. Every widget has at least one plain form, which is how every style can show every widget.

The builder's Preview in row redraws your layout in any style, including the ones you haven't bought yet.

Readable on any background

This was the hardest part. A widget can land anywhere, and the backgrounds run from dark walnut to almost-white glass. In testing, the text of one widget vanished in Rally, and another drew white on nearly white.

We didn't want shadows or boxes behind every number, so the app now measures the tone of each background and dial. A widget keeps its colour only if it stands out at a contrast of at least 3 to 1. If it doesn't, it falls back, in order, to the colour you picked, the style's colour, the dial's own print colour, the display colour, and finally plain white or black. Panels that text can't be read on are recoloured too.

Reviewing our own layouts

With a builder in your hands, our built-in layouts became examples, and some were poor ones. An audit of all 52 layouts on 27 October listed 194 problems, big and small, and only two layouts came through clean. Over six review rounds we removed the weakest built-in and ready-made layouts, made Clockmaker, Commute, Road Trip and Grand Tour mirror-symmetric, and added three for tablets and foldables: Grand dash, Wide panel and Fold music. The music card also stopped being one plain box: it is now drawn as each style's own display. All of it shipped in version 2.0.8, released on 30 October.

We also set a rule for ourselves: a layout we make reaches the market only after it has been reviewed by eye.

Sharing a layout

Publishing to the market needs a free VHub sign-in. Each published layout gets a six-character code, made only of letters and digits that can't be mistaken for one another, and a link that opens it in the app. Anyone can rate a layout from one to five stars, but only after using it, and never their own. Three open reports hide a layout until we have looked at it. The layouts people have shared are on the site, each drawn by the app itself.
