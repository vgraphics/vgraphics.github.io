One clock, not two: reworking the guardian fights in Exitwise™

3 November 2026

The last level of every world in Exitwise™ is a guardian fight. Until the end of October, those fights put two clocks on the screen at the same time. A screenshot of level 160 made the problem plain. This post is about how two clocks became one, and about a lost heart whose real cause wasn't the one we blamed.

How a guardian fight works

There are 17 guardians. Bramble waits at the end of the first world, and the last one stands at level 500. You still clear the board by tapping arrows that can fly free, but an arrow that leaves through the top edge also hits the guardian, and the last hit breaks its curse. The curse runs on a clock. Each hit wins a few seconds back, and if the clock runs out, the attempt is lost.

Across part of the top edge sits the ward, a stone shield. Fly an arrow into it and you lose a heart. After a set number of hits, four for most guardians, the ward moves. Later guardians add their own tricks, such as frost that drops numbered ice onto the board, or ink that hides arrows.

Two clocks on one screen

Every level in Exitwise has a play timer in the stats row at the top, counting up. Your time decides your stars and your best times. The guardian fights added a second clock inside the guardian's strip, counting down. Both were right, but only one of them could end the fight, and in the screenshot above, the one your eye meets first is the one that can't.

We drew three ways out. In the first, the curse clock takes the play timer's place in the stats row and the strip loses its clock. In the second, the strip keeps its clock and the timer hides. In the third, play time stays and the curse becomes a burning fuse along the strip. We went with the first. The stats row already counts down in Coin Rush, so players know that spot as the one that can run out, and play time still runs underneath, ready for the pause and win cards, so stars and records didn't change.

Making one clock say enough

A single clock has to do the work of two, so it changes as time runs low. It turns amber under 30 seconds. Under 10 it turns red and swells a little with every tick you hear. A thin bar under the digits drains as the curse closes in, and every hit throws a small +7 s, or whatever that guardian gives, up above the digits, so you can see the time you just won back. This went out in version 1.0.24 on 28 October.

Ice that looked the same

The same update fixed something smaller. Ice dropped by frost and spells picks a number, and it often picked one already on the board: one run of level 220 ended up with three blocks reading 12. Blocks that read the same are hard to tell apart, and three in a row looked like a mistake. New ice now keeps its number at least three apart from the ice already in play.

The heart we blamed on the wrong thing

On level 160 we twice lost a heart in a way that felt unfair. Both times an extra arrow from Double-Shot was involved, so we blamed it, and the first fix was aimed at Double-Shot.

Before going further, we measured. We ran the real level 300 times with a simulated player and logged every heart lost to the ward. The real cause was timing. A hit that moves the ward can drop it onto the half of the edge you're working in, and the tap you had already lined up flies straight into it. Those 300 runs had 98 taps like that, most of them right after the player's own hit.

We looked at three answers: warn before the ward moves, stop extra hits from moving it, or let the ward never cost a heart. We chose the warning, in a form that needs no reading ahead: the bump itself is the warning. Since version 1.0.28 on 29 October, your first bump after the ward moves is free. It costs no heart, doesn't count as a mistake or add to the guardian's rage, and your combo keeps going. A short message tells you what happened: “The ward moved! First bump free.” The second bump costs a heart as before.

What we took from it

Both changes came from playing our own game on a hard level and paying attention to what felt wrong. In both, the feeling was right and our first explanation wasn't. Now, when a level feels unfair, we measure before we fix.
